CHATTER — EPA Criteria Pollutants by Location
EPA Criteria Pollutants by Location, grouped by parish

The Clean Air Act identifies 6 air pollutants that pose the greatest health risks. They are known as criteria pollutants. This section tracks four EPA criteria pollutants — sulfur dioxide (SO₂), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), ozone (O₃), and fine particle pollution (PM₂.₅) — measured at the state-run (LDEQ) regulatory monitors across Louisiana that report to EPA’s Air Quality System. Design values are specific to a monitoring location, so there is one card per monitor, and the cards are grouped under parish headers. Every card shows all four criteria pollutants, with the ones a monitor does not measure marked as such, so a location can be read as a complete picture of its criteria-pollutant concerns. Parishes are arranged by region — the New Orleans area first, then the Capital/Baton Rouge area, Acadiana, the southwest, central and north Louisiana — so neighbouring locations sit together.

The EPA uses air monitoring data to determine if pollution levels meet the Clean Air Act requirements. They make these decisions based on a number representing all the measurements over a year taken at an air monitor. It is called a Design Value. It represents the highest pollution levels in that area. In the figure, each cell shows the EPA Design Value for that pollutant divided by the corresponding National Ambient Air Quality Standard (NAAQS). A value below 1.00 means the design value is within the standard; values above 1.00 indicate the yearly average is greater than the Clean Air Act (NAAQS) standard and poses an increased health risk. Design values are computed using the same statistics EPA uses to determine if air pollution levels in the region are meeting the criteria set by the Clean Air Act. The calendar-year columns are EPA’s own published single-year values for each monitor, taken from EPA’s design value reports; the year-to-date column, the rolling Last 365 Days window and the Last Available Month — periods EPA has not published yet — are calculated by CHATTER from EPA’s daily monitoring data in the same way. EPA AQS releases data on a delay of weeks to months, so each of those three headings names, on its second line, the month the criteria data actually ends in. The color scale is shared across all locations, so cards can be compared directly.

Not every monitor measures every pollutant. Hatched gray cells mean no measurement exists — a pollutant that is not monitored at this site is shown as an empty hatched row rather than being hidden, so a gap in coverage is never mistaken for a clean reading.

How to read this plot (click to expand)

Each row is one of the EPA criteria pollutants measured here — nitrogen dioxide (NO₂), sulfur dioxide (SO₂), ozone (O₃) and fine particles (PM₂.₅); the Clean Air Act names six, and four are measured at these sites. Each column is a period of time. Unlike the other pages, the number here is not compared to a screening level — it is the EPA design value divided by the National Ambient Air Quality Standard (NAAQS), written as a multiple with an “x.”

A design value is the particular statistic EPA uses for each pollutant when comparing a monitor against its standard: the annual average for NO₂ and PM₂.₅, the fourth-highest daily maximum 8-hour average for O₃, and the 99th percentile of the daily 1-hour maxima for SO₂. (The FAQ lists the standard used for each pollutant.) Below 1.00x means the pollutant is within the standard; above 1.00x means it is above the standard.

Design values are specific to a monitoring location, so there is one card per monitor. The cards are grouped under parish headers — each header names the parish and lists its monitors — and the parishes are arranged by region (the New Orleans area first, then the Capital/Baton Rouge area, Acadiana, the southwest, central and north Louisiana), so neighbouring locations sit together. Where a parish has several monitors, compare their cards directly: they often measure different pollutants.

A clarifying example.

Under the Jefferson Parish header, on the Kenner heatmap, find the row for O₃ (ozone) and the column for 2025. The square reads 0.94x — Kenner’s ozone design value for 2025 was 6% below the national standard. In the same column, NO₂ reads 0.09x, well under the annual standard, and PM₂.₅ reads 0.87x — EPA’s 2025 annual average of about 7.8 µg/m³, below the annual standard of 9.0 µg/m³.

Where the standards are reached. Ozone sat at or above the standard in 2023 or 2024 at several Baton Rouge-area monitors — Carville and Port Allen at 1.10x in 2023, LSU at 1.06x in both years, Capitol at 1.06x in 2023 — and below it in 2025. Fine particles, compared with the annual standard, were above it in 2023 at Westlake (Calcasieu, 1.12x), Shreveport / Calumet (Caddo, 1.10x), Houma (Terrebonne, 1.06x), Port Allen (1.04x) and Capitol (1.03x), and below it everywhere in 2025. In 2026 so far (through September) fine particles are above the annual standard at Monroe / Airport (Ouachita, 1.04x; 1.13x in September alone) and Port Allen (1.02x) — partial-year values that will move as the year fills in. Sulfur dioxide and nitrogen dioxide stayed within their standards at every monitor in the years EPA has published.

Where the numbers come from. The calendar-year columns are EPA’s own published single-year values (an asterisk marks a year EPA says does not meet its completeness requirements); the year-to-date, Last 365 Days and monthly columns, which EPA has not published yet, are calculated by CHATTER from EPA’s daily data. A note under each heatmap says which is which.

This page runs later than the other two. The criteria monitors report continuously and reach EPA’s database within weeks, while the canister results behind the cancer-risk and other-health-risk pages arrive months later, so the criteria page shows more recent years and its year-to-date, Last 365 Days and monthly columns end at a later date. The last three columns name their ending month. EPA AQS, the source for this page, releases data weeks to months after the fact, so the year-to-date, Last 365 Days and monthly columns here each carry, on a second line, the month the criteria data actually ends in — for example “Last 365 Days (ending Sep 2026)” is the 365 days up to the end of September. A site whose own data ends earlier than that month shows a blank cell rather than older data.

Hatched gray rows labeled “not monitored at this site” mean that monitor does not measure that pollutant at all. Coverage is uneven across the state: most monitors measure only ozone or only fine particles; nitrogen dioxide is measured at just a handful of sites, and sulfur dioxide mainly in the industrial river parishes.

One caution. These are single-year values. EPA’s official design values, the ones used to decide whether an area meets the Clean Air Act standard, average three consecutive years to make a decision, ensuring that a single stand-out year does not unduly influence compliance determinations. So a value above 1.00x here is a signal worth a closer look, not an official finding that the area violated the standard.
