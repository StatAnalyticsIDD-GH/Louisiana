CHATTER — Data, Methods & FAQ
Where the numbers come from, and how they are calculated

Where the numbers come from. CHATTER does not measure anything itself. Every value on this site is computed from public monitoring records kept by two agencies.

The cancer-risk and other-health-risk pollutants come from the Louisiana Department of Environmental Quality’s (LDEQ) canister network: at each site a 24-hour air sample is collected every sixth day and analyzed in a laboratory for a fixed list of volatile organic compounds. Results through 2023 are taken from EPA’s Ambient Monitoring Archive for Hazardous Air Pollutants, the national compilation LDEQ reports to; results from 2024 onward are downloaded directly from LDEQ’s canister-data portal, because the national archive is published with a lag of a year or more. The two sources report the same samples in the same units, and CHATTER stitches them into one record per monitor.

The EPA criteria pollutants — ozone (O₃), fine particles (PM₂.₅), nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) and sulfur dioxide (SO₂) — come from the continuous regulatory monitors LDEQ operates across the state, whose hourly and daily readings are retrieved from EPA’s Air Quality System (AQS). These monitors report within weeks, so the criteria page usually runs several months later than the canister tabs.

How a heatmap cell is calculated. For a canister pollutant, CHATTER takes every valid sample in the period (a calendar year, the year to date, the last 365 days, or the most recent complete calendar quarter), averages them, and divides that mean by the pollutant’s EPA screening level — the concentration below which long-term exposure is not expected to raise health concerns. A cell of 1.00x means the average sat exactly at the screening level. Cancer-risk cells use the cancer screening level; other-health-risk cells use the non-cancer screening level for the same compound, and a health-effect total adds up the ratios of every pollutant linked to that effect.

For a criteria pollutant the cell is a single-period design value — the particular statistic EPA compares against each National Ambient Air Quality Standard, such as the fourth-highest daily maximum eight-hour ozone concentration, or the annual average for nitrogen dioxide and fine particles — divided by that standard. For completed years CHATTER shows the single-year value EPA itself publishes in its design value reports; for the year to date, the last 365 days and the last month, which EPA has not published, CHATTER calculates the same statistic from EPA’s daily data. EPA’s official design values average three years; the values shown here are for one period at a time, so they signal where a closer look is warranted rather than declare an area in or out of attainment.

Two marks show when a number rests on thinner ground. An asterisk means that only 60–75 % of the scheduled samples were available for that period (below 60 % the cell is left blank). A double asterisk means that most of the samples behind the number were below the laboratory’s detection limit; such samples are counted at half the detection limit, so the true value is uncertain and could be anywhere from near zero up to that limit.

Sources

The standards, screening levels and health effects CHATTER uses come from EPA, and the measurements from LDEQ and EPA monitoring records.

  • EPA National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) — the standards the criteria-pollutant heat maps are compared with: EPA, NAAQS Table, https://www.epa.gov/criteria-air-pollutants/naaqs-table.
  • EPA Air Quality Design Values — EPA’s published single-year values used for the completed years of the criteria-pollutant heat maps: EPA, Air Quality Design Values, https://www.epa.gov/air-trends/air-quality-design-values (reports for 2023–2025, June 2026).
  • EPA Regional Screening Levels (RSLs) — the cancer and non-cancer screening levels for residential air that the cancer-risk and other-health-risk heat maps are compared with: EPA, Regional Screening Levels (RSLs) – Generic Tables, https://www.epa.gov/risk/regional-screening-levels-rsls-generic-tables.
  • EPA Integrated Risk Information System (IRIS) — the health effects listed beside each pollutant: EPA, Integrated Risk Information System, https://www.epa.gov/iris; assessments by chemical at https://iris.epa.gov/AtoZ/?list_type=alpha (for example benzene and 1,3-butadiene).
  • LDEQ canister monitoring — 24-hour hazardous-air-pollutant samples (EPA method TO-15) and continuous criteria-pollutant monitors, Louisiana Department of Environmental Quality, reported to EPA’s Air Quality System; network description at https://www.deq.louisiana.gov/page/ambient-air-monitoring-program.
  • EPA Air Quality System (AQS) — canister results and daily criteria-pollutant values, retrieved through the AQS API: https://aqs.epa.gov/aqsweb/documents/data_api.html.
Download the data

Every value on this site, in one file. CHATTER_Louisiana_values.csv holds one row for every heatmap cell — monitor, pollutant (or health effect, or total), and period — together with the summary statistics it was computed from. It is regenerated from the same code that draws the pages, so a row in the file cannot disagree with a cell on the site. It opens in Excel, R or Python; about 3,500 rows.

Each row carries the group (Cancer Risk, Other Health Risks, EPA Criteria Pollutants), the monitor and its AQS site number, the parish, the period and its calendar bounds, and value_shown, the number in the cell. For canister pollutants it adds the mean concentration in ppb, the screening level it was divided by, the number of valid samples and the number scheduled, the resulting coverage and whether the cell carries the asterisk, and the share of the readings that were detection-limit substitutes and whether the cell carries the double asterisk. For criteria pollutants it adds the design value in the standard’s own units, the standard, the statistic used, and the number of daily values behind it. Totals are marked as such and carry only the displayed sum.

How each statistic is defined. CHATTER_statistics_documentation.xlsx is the reference sheet: every statistic that appears on the site, its formula, and where in the code it is computed.

Technical details
Screening levels, data completeness, periods, design values (click to expand)

Screening levels. EPA Regional Screening Levels, Resident Air table, November 2023 edition, converted from µg/m³ to ppb at 24.45 L/mol; cancer values at a 10⁻⁶ target risk, non-cancer values at a hazard quotient of 1. Two compounds added for Louisiana (trichloroethylene and vinyl chloride) have not yet been assigned a non-cancer health-effect category and appear under “Health effect not yet assigned” on the other-health-risk pages.

Sampling schedule and coverage. Canister monitors follow a one-in-six-day schedule. Scheduled samples for a period are counted from each monitor’s first sample date in six-day steps; coverage is valid samples divided by scheduled samples. Periods below 60 % coverage are not shown; 60–75 % are shown with an asterisk.

Periods. Calendar years, the year to date, a rolling 365 days, and the most recent complete calendar quarter, all measured back from the data cutoff. For the canister pages the cutoff is the latest day on which any monitor has a sample, so a monitor whose results arrive later shows reduced coverage in its recent periods rather than holding the whole site back. The criteria page runs to its own last day of data and says so in its column headings.

Non-detects. A result below the laboratory’s method detection limit is counted at half that limit. For 2024-onward portal downloads, which report non-detects as zero without a detection limit, the most recent detection limit reported for that monitor and compound in the archive is used. When at least half of the summed readings behind a cell are such substitutes, the cell carries the double-asterisk mark.

Statistic displayed. The arithmetic mean (average) of the period’s samples. A 95 % upper confidence limit of the mean (Student’s t) is also computed and kept in the working tables but is not displayed.

Criteria pollutants. Completed calendar years are EPA’s published single-year values for each site, from EPA’s Air Quality Design Values reports (https://www.epa.gov/air-trends/air-quality-design-values): the PM₂.₅ annual mean, the NO₂ annual design value, the ozone fourth-highest daily maximum 8-hour average and the SO₂ 99th percentile of daily 1-hour maxima; a year EPA marks as not meeting its completeness requirements carries an asterisk. The periods EPA has not published (year to date, last 365 days, last month) are calculated by CHATTER from AQS daily values, primary monitors only; where a site runs two instruments for the same pollutant the higher daily value is used. Each pollutant is compared with one standard from EPA’s NAAQS table, in that standard’s own form: nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) — the mean of the daily averages against the annual standard of 53 ppb; fine particles (PM₂.₅, parameter 88101) — the mean of the daily averages against the annual primary standard of 9.0 µg/m³; ozone (O₃) — the fourth-highest daily maximum 8-hour average against 0.070 ppm; sulfur dioxide (SO₂) — the 99th percentile of the daily 1-hour maxima against the 1-hour primary standard of 75 ppb. EPA’s annual PM₂.₅ mean averages four quarterly means and applies completeness rules; CHATTER’s calculated periods use the plain mean of the daily values.

Code. The site is a Quarto project; every table is built by one Python module per pollutant group, and the pages only draw them. The file above is written by the same modules.

Frequently Asked Questions
Q: How do I read a CHATTER heat map?

A: Most of the plots in CHATTER are heat maps. A heat map is a grid of squares where the number in each square is the measurement, and the color of the square tells you at a glance how that number compares to a health-based limit.

Each row is an air pollutant (or, on some pages, a health effect or a total). Each column is a period of time — a calendar year, the year so far, the most recent 365 days, or the most recent complete quarter.

The number in each square is a multiple, written with an “x.” It is the measured level divided by the limit — the equation is written under the name at the top of every heat map. So 1.00x means the level is exactly at the limit. A number below 1.00x means the level is below the limit — for example, 0.50x is half the limit. A number above 1.00x means the level is above the limit — for example, 3.00x means the level is three times the limit, and 6.50x means six and a half times the limit.

The color scale beside each heat map shows what those multiples mean. The palest color means the level is below the limit. The darker the color, the further above the limit the level is. The label on the scale sits at the line where each band begins, so a square colored in the band starting at “5x” is somewhere between five and ten times the limit.

The bottom row of some plots is a Total. It adds up the individual pollutants above it, to show the combined burden for that time period.

For a worked example using real numbers, open the “How to read this plot” panel at the top of any of the plot pages.

Q: What do the gray squares and the asterisks mean?

A: Squares shaded gray with diagonal hatching mean there is no measurement for that pollutant in that period — usually because the monitor does not measure it, or too few readings were recorded. A gap in the data is not the same as a low reading, so CHATTER marks it differently rather than leaving it blank.

An asterisk after a number (for example 1.72x*) means the period had between 60% and 75% of its scheduled samples, so the average rests on thinner data. Periods with less than 60% are left blank. A double asterisk before a number (for example **1.20x) means most of the samples behind it were below the laboratory’s detection limit — see the questions on how the pollutants are measured below.

Q: What is an air pollution monitor?

A: An air pollution monitor is an instrument that measures the pollutants in the outdoor air to understand air quality conditions. See EPA’s overview of ambient air monitoring.

Q: How are the air pollutants in CHATTER Louisiana measured?

A: The hazardous air pollutants — the cancer risk and other health risk groups — are measured by the Louisiana Department of Environmental Quality (LDEQ) with canister samples: a stainless-steel canister draws in outdoor air for 24 hours, and the sample is sent to a laboratory that identifies and measures each chemical (EPA method TO-15). LDEQ takes one such sample every sixth day, so a year has about sixty samples at each site. The results are reported to EPA’s Air Quality System, which is where CHATTER reads them. The EPA criteria pollutants are measured continuously, hour by hour, at LDEQ’s regulatory monitors.

This is different from the continuous hourly instruments used in some other CHATTER communities, and it changes how the tracker judges completeness: instead of asking what share of the hours in a period had a reading, CHATTER Louisiana asks what share of the scheduled samples were taken.

Q: What does the double asterisk (**) in front of a number mean?

A: A laboratory can only measure a chemical down to a certain concentration — its detection limit. When a sample contains less than that, the laboratory reports it as “not detected.” CHATTER counts such a sample at half the detection limit, a common convention that neither ignores the sample nor assumes the chemical was absent.

For a few chemicals — vinyl chloride and 1,2-dichloroethane in particular, and 1,3-butadiene before 2023 — the detection limit is itself above the cancer screening level, so a value made up mostly of non-detects is set by the laboratory’s detection limit rather than by the air. CHATTER puts a double asterisk in front of any value in which half or more of the summed readings are detection-limit substitutes. It marks the number as uncertain: the true concentration could be anything from near zero up to the detection limit. It is not an upper bound — the real level could be higher or lower than the number shown; we simply cannot tell from samples the laboratory could not measure.

Q: Do all air monitors measure the same air pollutants?

A: No. Some air monitors measure only a few air pollutants compared to others. The state and EPA decide which instruments are placed where and which pollutants they will measure. You can find LDEQ’s description of its monitoring network on the LDEQ Ambient Air Monitoring Program page and its current readings at airquality.deq.louisiana.gov. You can also find monitors that are operated in collaboration with the federal government on the EPA AirNow website.

Q: What air pollutants are included in CHATTER?

A: CHATTER includes air pollutants measured at local air monitors for which EPA has published a health screening level and information on the body system or health outcome that is affected by the pollutant. Examples of health systems or outcomes the pollutant impacts are leukemia (a cancer), lung, liver, and the immune system. CHATTER also includes four of the six common pollutants regulated by the Clean Air Act — the four measured near this community.

Q: Why are some air pollutants not included in CHATTER?

A: There are several reasons why an air pollutant may not be in CHATTER.

CHATTER includes only hazardous air pollutants that LDEQ routinely measures at its canister sampling sites and for which EPA has published health screening levels. LDEQ’s laboratory analysis covers about 45 chemicals, but many are almost never detected in the air; CHATTER shows the fourteen that are measured every year and either have a cancer screening level or are found at meaningful levels. Some hazardous air pollutants are not included because they are not routinely monitored by the state, or because they do not have the information needed to evaluate potential health impacts.

CHATTER also only shows a value when at least 60% of the scheduled samples in a period were taken, and marks values between 60% and 75% with an asterisk. Sometimes a monitor has data missing over time. When too many samples are missing, this can lead to error or too much uncertainty about the measurements that were not included.

Q: What is an EPA screening level?

A: An EPA screening level is a reference point, based on scientific studies, that helps identify when air pollution may be high enough to raise health concerns and warrants added action or investigation. CHATTER compares measured air pollution levels to these screening levels and shows when pollution levels exceed them.

Depending on the amount by which a pollutant exceeds the screening level, further action may be warranted to reduce people’s exposure to unhealthy levels of outdoor air pollution. For measurements falling below the level, no further action may be warranted. Exceeding a screening level does not mean that health effects will occur. Instead, it indicates that the pollution level may require closer evaluation and, in some cases, actions to reduce exposure.

EPA provides screening levels for both cancer risk and other health effects, such as breathing problems, heart disease, and other chronic health conditions. CHATTER compares the average air pollution level over each period with the EPA screening level. The screening levels CHATTER uses are EPA’s Regional Screening Levels (RSLs) for residential air.

Q: How does EPA set screening levels for pollutants that pose a cancer risk?

A: EPA uses past scientific studies to understand cancer risks related to air pollution levels over a person’s lifetime. EPA generally considers a lifetime cancer risk of one additional cancer case per one million people exposed over 70 years to be a low level of concern. Risks above this level suggest closer evaluations are needed, while risks approaching 100 additional cancer cases per one million people exposed over 70 years are generally considered a greater concern and may require actions to reduce exposure.

A lifetime cancer risk of one in a million means that if one million people were exposed to the same level of a pollutant continuously for 70 years, one additional person may develop cancer because of that exposure.

CHATTER uses EPA’s one-in-a-million increased cancer case air pollution screening levels for residents. In the heat map scale, the palest color means the air pollution level is below the one-added-cancer-case-per-million-people screening level. The darker the color, the higher the value compared to the screening level threshold. Air pollution levels 100x the screening level are shown in the darkest orange. The 100x screening level means increased risk of 100 cancer cases in one million people if they were exposed over a 70-year lifespan.

Q: How does EPA set screening levels for pollutants that pose health impacts other than cancer?

A: The EPA also sets screening levels for long-term health outcomes that are not cancer. EPA uses scientific studies to set screening levels for air pollution levels that may pose a risk of long-term health problems other than cancer, such as breathing problems, heart disease, and other chronic health conditions. Air pollution levels are acceptable when they are equal to or below the screening level, and suggest further action when they are ten times greater than the screening level.

Q: Where do the health effects listed beside each pollutant come from?

A: The health effect listed for each pollutant — for example leukemia for benzene and 1,3-butadiene, or the urinary system — follows EPA’s Integrated Risk Information System (IRIS), which assesses how exposure to environmental contaminants may affect health. Each chemical’s IRIS assessment can be found in the IRIS list of substances.

Q: What is a National Ambient Air Quality Standard?

A: National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) are air quality standards set by EPA under the Clean Air Act for six common pollutants: ozone, fine particles, carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, nitrogen dioxide, and lead. These standards are designed to help protect public health, including for sensitive groups such as children, older adults, and people with heart or lung disease. Air pollution measurements from monitoring stations are compared to these standards to determine whether an area’s air quality meets federal health-based requirements. The standards are listed in EPA’s NAAQS Table.

Q: What is an EPA design value, and why does CHATTER use one for the criteria pollutants?

A: A design value is the specific statistic EPA uses to compare a monitor’s measurements against a National Ambient Air Quality Standard. Each standard is written for a specific averaging time and a specific statistic. For example, the ozone standard is based on the fourth-highest daily maximum 8-hour average in a year, while the annual fine-particle (PM₂.₅) standard is based on the annual average. So a simple yearly average is the right comparison for some standards but not for others. CHATTER calculates each pollutant using the form EPA specifies for the standard it is compared with:

Pollutant Standard used (NAAQS) How the design value is calculated
Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) 53 ppb (1-year; primary and secondary) Annual mean
Ozone (O₃) 0.070 ppm (8-hour; primary and secondary) Annual fourth-highest daily maximum 8-hour average concentration
Fine particles (PM₂.₅) 9.0 µg/m³ (1-year; primary) Annual mean
Sulfur dioxide (SO₂) 75 ppb (1-hour; primary) 99th percentile of 1-hour daily maximum concentrations

Each cell in the criteria pollutant heat maps shows the design value divided by the standard. A value below 1.00x means the pollutant is within the standard; a value above 1.00x means it is above the standard.

One important limitation. EPA’s official design values, the ones used to formally determine whether an area meets a standard, are averaged over three consecutive years of monitoring. CHATTER shows each year separately: the completed-year columns are the single-year values EPA publishes in its design value reports, and the year-to-date, last-365-days and last-month columns, which EPA has not published yet, are calculated by CHATTER from EPA’s daily monitoring data. This makes year-to-year changes visible, which is the point of the tracker, but it means a CHATTER value above 1.00x is not the same as an official EPA finding that an area has violated the standard. Treat it as a signal worth a closer look, not as a regulatory determination.

Q: What statistics are used to represent the air pollution level in CHATTER?

A: It depends on the group of pollutants, because EPA compares each group to its standard in a different way.

For the cancer risk pollutants and the other health risk pollutants, CHATTER uses the average of the 24-hour samples in the period (for example, the yearly average) to approximate the level of pollution people are exposed to over a longer period. This is a health-protective way to summarize a year of monitoring, and it is consistent with EPA methods for long-term exposure.

For the EPA criteria pollutants, CHATTER uses the statistic that each National Ambient Air Quality Standard is written for — the design value described above. For nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) and fine particles (PM₂.₅) that is the annual average; for ozone (O₃) and sulfur dioxide (SO₂) it is a high value from the year, as shown in the table above.
